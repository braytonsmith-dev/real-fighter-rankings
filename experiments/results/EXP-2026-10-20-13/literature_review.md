# Literature review: separating the "done" older fighter from the still-elite older fighter

Prepared for the REAL Fighter Rankings project, 20 October 2026, by a research assistant run under the project owner's direction (AI-assisted; every citation was opened and checked unless flagged in the verification notes).

## 1. Aging curves in combat sports and transferable methods

Population peak ages. The only systematic review of peak competitive age, Allen and Hopkins [1], found that peak age falls with event duration in explosive or sprint events (about 27 years for athletics throws down to about 20 for sprint swimming) and rises with duration in endurance events (about 20 for swimming up to about 39 for ultra-distance cycling). For mixed or skill-dominated sports, which is where MMA sits, they report insufficient data to fit a trend. In baseball, Bradbury [2] used 86 MLB seasons and multiple regression with player controls and estimated an overall peak near 29, with athletic skills (hitting, running) peaking earlier than knowledge skills such as drawing walks. Berry, Reese and Larkey [4] fitted hierarchical Bayesian models to 1,136 NHL forwards, 488 golfers and 7,031 MLB non-pitchers and found peaks of 27 (hockey points), 27 (batting average), 29 (home runs) and 34 (golf); hockey showed a sharp decline after 30 (a 34-year-old at roughly 75 percent of peak) while golf declined gradually. Brander, Egan and Yeung [5] used fixed-effects regressions on NHL seasons 1997-2012 and found scoring peaks at 27-28 for forwards and 28-29 for defencemen, with a wide near-peak plateau (24-32 and 24-34) and implausible results when selection bias was ignored. Vaci and colleagues [6] fitted a Bayesian latent-factor model with player-specific pre-peak and post-peak slopes to 2,845 NBA players over about 50 years: inflection points fell around 28-29, individual inflection points ranged from 24 to 33, and players with steeper development showed shallower decline (the strongest developers barely declined at all). Berthelot and colleagues [7] fitted a Moore-type exponential growth-then-decline curve to 646 track careers, 512 swimming careers and 96 chess grandmasters, obtaining mean peaks of 26.0 (track), 21.0 (swimming) and 31.4 (chess) with adjusted R squared of 0.997 and 0.998 for the two athletic series. Kovalchik [8] documented that the average age of the ATP top 100 rose about 0.34 years per season from the mid-2000s to 27.9 in 2012, with players aged 30 and over making up 29 percent of the top 100, a warning that peak age is not fixed across eras.

Gradual or cliff-like? Fair's masters-record analysis [3] finds near-linear loss of roughly 0.6 percent per year in sprints and 0.8 percent per year in distance running from age 35, with acceleration only in the seventies; chess loses about 0.2 percent per year. Individual curves in team sports look more like a plateau followed by an accelerating drop [4, 6, 12]. Griffin, Hinoveanu and Hopker [9], with a Bayesian hierarchical model on 95,376 men's and 48,999 women's 100 m results, found individual trajectories and peak ages that "vary considerably" around the population curve, which is the central methodological point for this project.

Methods. The literature uses four families: the delta method (year-over-year differences of the same athlete), which Schuckers, Lopez and Macdonald [11] show is biased by selective survival and is outperformed by spline models with player fixed effects or imputation; fixed-effects regressions with quadratic age [2, 5]; Bayesian hierarchical or mixed models with player random slopes [4, 6, 9]; and functional data analysis of whole careers [12, 13]. Nguyen and Matthews [10] show that imputing the unobserved seasons of players who left MLB moves the estimated peak from 30 to 26 and lowers the whole late-career curve, i.e., survivor bias flatters older athletes. Cavan, Cao and Swartz [12] estimate individual NHL curves by FPCA with an explicit selection-bias adjustment and find faster decline than conventional estimates. Career-end survival analysis exists in baseball and basketball: Witnauer, Rogers and Saint Onge [72] (5,989 MLB position players, 1902-1993) report a mean career of 5.6 years and an exit probability of at least 11 percent at every career stage; Groothuis and Hill [70] fit semiparametric duration models to NBA careers and find performance the dominant determinant of exit.

Combat sports. In judo, Franchini, Fukuda and Lopes-Silva [14] tracked 12,005 athletes across 16 World Championships and 6 Olympics: heavier athletes were older, Olympians were older than World Championship entrants, and early-round losers were younger among women; Kons and colleagues [15] report a median age of 27 for Paris 2024 Olympic judoka. In wrestling, Latyshev and colleagues [16] (332 world-championship top-eight finishers, 2017-2019) found a mean age of 26.5 (SD 3.6), medalists 26.3, a fitted quadratic peak near 24-26, 67.7 percent of medalists aged 22-28, and older means in heavier groups (25.9, 26.6, 27.2). Boxing has thin academic coverage: Warnick and Warnick [17] (400 BoxRec contests) found age, cumulative wins and losses, and the previous result predicted victory; Fiala and colleagues [18] (Bayesian logistic regression on BoxRec heavyweights) found age and height had far stronger effects on win probability than body mass; Clausen, McCrory and Anderson [19] documented that mean career exposure fell from about 336 bouts over 19 years in the 1930s to about 13 bouts over 5 years by 2002. No peer-reviewed boxing peak-age curve was found.

MMA-specific. Kirk's three studies are the core peer-reviewed evidence. In 278 bouts [20] older fighters lost more often, older losers lost disproportionately by strikes, and decision winners were older than KO or submission winners. In 2,229 bouts over five years [22] winners averaged 29.8 (SD 4.0) versus losers 30.7 (SD 4.2), a relative gap of 0.82 years, and age separated winners from losers in 4 of 13 divisions. His ranking study [21] (Fight Matrix top 100 per division, January 2018) found heavyweights oldest (32.8, SD 5.3) and bantamweights youngest (29.0, SD 4.1), with the top 20 older (32.3) than ranks 81-100 (29.9) and only middleweight showing a within-division age-rank correlation: elite MMA is old relative to winning MMA, a survivorship signature. Hutchison and colleagues [23] (844 UFC bouts, 2006-2012) identified older age as a risk factor for both KO and TKO. Non-academic analyses on FightMetric or UFCStats data agree on the gradient: Harrington [25] (880 UFC bouts with known ages, 2010) found the younger fighter won 53 percent overall, 55.4 percent with a 5-10 year gap and 58.3 percent above 10 years; the Luke Thomas 2012 analysis of about 2,600 fights, as reported by Fighters Only [24], found the younger fighter won 57 percent, rising from about 50 percent at equal age to about 70 percent at a 10-year gap, and fighters aged 35 and over won 2 of 33 title fights at welterweight and below; AgentMMA [27] (8,305 UFC bouts, 2005-2026) reports win rates of 57.6 percent at 24 and under, 52.2 percent at 28-30, 48.0 percent at 31-33, 42.7 percent at 34-36 and 37.6 percent at 37 and over, with the younger fighter winning 67.1 percent when 8 or more years younger. Wyman [26] (48 careers) placed the onset of decline about 9.5 years after debut, mean age 31.7 (SD 3.5). The project's finding (favourite 5 or more years younger wins 70 percent, 5 or more years older wins 41 percent) is therefore a replication with better controls, not a new fact; the drop between the 31-33 and 34-36 bands in [27] is the closest public evidence that the MMA curve steepens rather than sloping evenly.

## 2. Damage accumulation and chin degradation

Clinical exposure effects. Martland [29] described parkinsonian "punch drunk" boxers in 1928. Jordan and colleagues [31] (30 boxers) found mean chronic brain injury scores of 2.6 in high-exposure boxers (12 or more bouts) versus 0.3 in low-exposure boxers, with APOE e4 carriers scoring 3.9 versus 1.8 among the high-exposure group. The Professional Fighters Brain Health Study is the modern quantitative anchor: Bernick and colleagues [32] (224 fighters, 131 MMA and 93 boxers) built a Fight Exposure Score from total fights and fights per year; each additional professional fight was associated with 0.19 percent slower processing speed, a maximal score with 8.8 percent slower speed, and each score point with 0.8-0.9 percent smaller thalamic, caudate and amygdala volumes. Longitudinally [33] (204 participants, mean follow-up 2.3-3.0 years), active boxers lost left thalamic volume at about 102 mm3 per year and active MMA fighters at about 58 mm3 per year relative to controls, with boxers declining faster than MMA fighters after controlling for fights, and a "decliner" subgroup (caudate loss above a yearly threshold) showing worsening Trails A and B. The Association of Ringside Physicians consensus [41] prescribes minimum suspensions of 30 days after a head-strike TKO, 60 after a KO without loss of consciousness and 90 with it, which gives a defensible recency window for a damage feature. The UFC Performance Institute reports [42, 43] contain finish rates by division (heavyweight 60.1 percent KO/TKO versus women's strawweight 7.1 percent) and a concussion share of injuries (15.2 percent male, 9.2 percent female), but no aging curves or absorbed-strike longevity analysis.

Does prior damage predict later KO? The evidence is mixed and that is the opening. Hutchison [23] found a prior KO or TKO to be a risk factor for KO but not TKO. Hada and colleagues [34] (104 male professionals) found fighters with a KO-loss history had 2.3 times the odds of concussion in their latest bout, a 65.0 percent concussion rate with three or more prior KO losses, and 60.8 percent versus 37.0 percent for more than 30 bouts versus fewer. Albert and colleagues [35] (705 UFC athletes with a KO/TKO loss, 2015-2023) found 22.7 percent lost the next bout by KO/TKO and prior KO history was the only independent predictor (adjusted odds ratio 1.13, 95 percent CI 1.02-1.25), with age (31.8 versus 31.0) not independent after adjustment; these figures come from a secondary summary (see verification notes). Against this, Kotrba [36] (4,896 UFC fights, 2010-2021, 1,749 fighters) found the prior-KO count positively associated with KO loss in pooled logits (coefficient 0.249, odds ratio about 1.28) but negatively once fighter fixed effects were added, concluding a cumulative glass jaw "could not be proven" while about 18 percent of fighters (308) showed a significant individual KO propensity; betting odds were the strongest predictor of KO loss in every model. Bledsoe and colleagues [37] (171 Nevada bouts) found older age raised injury risk after adjustment. The pattern across [23, 34, 35, 36] is that fighter-level susceptibility dominates a simple damage count, which argues for random or fixed fighter effects rather than a raw tally. No study has yet used per-bout significant strikes absorbed, the exposure measure this project holds, as a longitudinal predictor of later KO loss or decline; the brain-health studies use fight counts [32, 33] and the head-strike studies [38, 40] are cross-sectional descriptions.

## 3. Style, activity and weight class

Style and longevity. No peer-reviewed test that grapplers age differently from strikers in MMA was found. Indirect evidence: Kirk [20] found older losers lost by strikes and older winners won by decision, consistent with striking defence degrading before grappling control; Bradbury [2] and Vaci [6] show skill-specific aging in other sports; Fernandes, de Brito, Brito, Aedo-Munoz and Miarka [44] (384 UFC athletes) and Ma [45] (174 ranked UFC athletes) show style determines finish method but did not test career length. Wyman [26] proposed strikes absorbed per minute as a longevity marker (Velasquez 1.56 versus Struve 3.96), without a formal test. Lam's thesis [46] (7,745 UFC fights) reports that winners absorb fewer significant strikes and that age difference is the strongest physical correlate (r = -0.19).

Activity and layoffs. Academic evidence on ring rust is absent. AgentMMA [28] (14,321 UFC appearances) found flat win rates (50.5-52.0 percent) for layoffs under 12 months but 42.4 percent for 1-2 years and 45.8 percent beyond 2 years, with the more active fighter winning 57.6 percent when the layoff gap exceeded 6 months; the author notes layoffs are not random (injury, suspension). Lam [46] includes days since last fight as a feature but does not report its weight.

Weight class. Heavier divisions are older [21, 14, 16] and more KO-prone. Hutchison [23] flags weight class as a KO and TKO risk factor; Follmer, Dellagrana and Zehr [38] (1,903 fights, 2014-2017) estimated KO/TKO odds relative to lightweight of 3.06 for heavyweight, 2.00 for light heavyweight, 1.80 for middleweight and 0.38 for flyweight; Manka-Malara and Mierzwinska-Nastalska [40] (2,488 numbered-event UFC fights, 2000-2021) measured 6.30 head strikes per minute (2.41 significant) with 31.6 percent of fights ended by head trauma. Higher finish rates mechanically raise outcome variance, so age effects at heavyweight will be noisier and the "older but still elite" heavyweight (Kirk's oldest division at 32.8) is both more common and harder to classify.

## 4. Predictive models with time-varying skill and covariates

Dynamic paired comparison. Fahrmeir and Tutz [49] and Glickman [47] cast Bradley-Terry strengths as a state-space process; Glickman [48] added stochastic volatility so a fighter's strength can jump (the Glicko-2 idea), applied to NFL and chess. Cattelan, Varin and Firth [51] used exponentially weighted past results. Coulom's Whole-History Rating [52] computes the exact MAP over every player's full rating history and reports better predictions than Elo, Glicko, TrueSkill and decayed history (no numbers on the project page). TrueSkill [53] and TrueSkill Through Time [54] smooth rather than filter skill series across 150 years of chess. Maystre, Kristof and Grossglauser [55] replaced static scores with Gaussian processes (KDD 2019): on ATP tennis, log loss 0.552 versus 0.563 for Elo and TrueSkill (accuracy 0.714 versus 0.705), with similar small gains in NBA, football and chess. Bong and colleagues [56] give a kernel-smoothed nonparametric dynamic Bradley-Terry.

Age inside the rating. Two papers do what the project needs. Araki, Hirose and Komaki [59] model each sumo wrestler's strength with piecewise quadratic age splines inside Thurstone-Mosteller and Bradley-Terry models and beat the standard models on AIC and Brier score. Baker and McHale [58] estimate deterministic age-dependent strengths for snooker players (peak 25-30) and then a random-effects model to test whether early promise is sustained; their tennis model [57] is the deterministic time-varying precursor.

Elo with covariates against the market. Kovalchik [60] compared 11 methods on 2,395 ATP matches: Elo reached 67-70 percent, bookmakers 72 percent, and "no model was able to beat the bookmakers". Vaughan Williams and colleagues [61] found surface-specific Elo competitive with odds at Grand Slams; Angelini, Candila and De Angelis [62] weighted Elo updates by scoreline over 60,000 matches and report the best forecasts and profitable betting. The project's 60.6 percent versus 67 percent market gap is therefore typical of the tennis literature, where the market lead is 2-5 points.

MMA models. Johnson [63] (3,638 FightMetric fights) reached 66.8 percent concordance in sample and 63.4 percent in cross-validation with logistic regression on strike differential per minute and knockdowns; no age or layoff terms. Holmes [64] built a Markov-chain simulator over positions with fighter-specific transition rates and reports predictions "comparable to the bookmakers" and positive betting returns. Berthet [65] summarises prior pre-fight accuracies at 50-71 percent and finds age significant in-fight (odds ratio 0.963 per year after round 1). Nichols, Mantro and Samuel [66] (UFC 2016-2022 train, 2023-2024 test) obtained 68.4 percent with crossed fighter random effects versus 67.8 percent for plain logistic and 66.7 percent for the market favourite, with age difference significant; the test is one or two seasons so the edge is not established. Lam [46] documents a well-calibrated closing market (Brier 0.216, expected calibration error 0.015). Kotrba [36] is the only MMA model with an explicit damage term. None of these models an individual decline trajectory.

## 5. Methods for finding the outlier

The individual-not-average toolkit has five pieces, each with a sports precedent. (a) Random slopes with shrinkage: Berry et al. [4] placed a bivariate normal on each player's maturing and declining slopes; Vaci et al. [6] estimated per-player post-peak slopes and found development predicted decline, with the population slope losing significance once player slopes entered; Griffin et al. [9] and Spyropoulou et al. [69] do the same in continuous time. (b) Age effects inside the strength model: [59] and [58]. (c) Functional PCA and clustering of whole careers: Wakim and Jin [13] found NBA early peakers (24.6) were the weakest cluster and late peakers (28.7) intermediate; Cavan et al. [12] add selection adjustment. (d) Changepoint detection with honest inference: Glazer [68] uses split-sample likelihood-ratio changepoints on MLB pitch-level metrics, flagging 10 of 11 known role changes and cutting false flags by about 88 percent; Bernick [33] uses a threshold on yearly caudate loss to define decliners. (e) Hazard models with performance covariates for career exit [70, 72], with the imputation and FPCA fixes for the survivor bias they create [10, 11, 12]. Kotrba's fighter fixed effects [36] are the MMA example of pulling individual susceptibility out of a population rate.

## 6. What would be new here

A credible contribution is an individual decline index estimated inside the rating model rather than beside it: a Bradley-Terry or Elo state with a per-fighter age slope (shrunk toward a division-level curve as in [4, 6, 59]) plus exposure covariates built from this dataset's per-bout stats (career significant head strikes absorbed, absorbed per minute trend, knockdowns against, KO/TKO losses with ARP-style recency weights [41], years active, fights per year as in [32]) and style shares (takedown and control time versus distance striking), validated walk-forward against closing odds. Nobody has combined per-bout absorbed strikes with age in a longitudinal outcome model; the brain-health literature uses fight counts [32, 33], the KO literature uses KO counts [34, 35, 36], and the rating literature uses age alone [58, 59]. Pitfalls: matchmaking selection (older fighters are booked against opponents chosen by the promoter, and Kotrba shows odds absorb most of the signal [36]); survivorship (the fighters still active at 38 are the ones who did not decline, which inflates late-career curves [10, 11, 21]); small samples (title fights at 35 and over number in the dozens [24], heavyweight outcomes are high variance [38, 42]); look-ahead (any feature using the current bout's strikes absorbed, or ages computed from later-revealed birthdates, leaks); era drift (strike rates doubled 2002-2017 [42], so absorbed-strike indices must be era-normalised); and the ARP recency windows interact with layoff selection [28].
